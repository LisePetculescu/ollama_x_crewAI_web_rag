# Maja's Copenhagen — The Spots I've Never Seen in a Tourist Guide

*By Maja Lindström · Local Guide Blog · Copenhagen*

---

I've lived in Copenhagen my entire life. All 40 years of it. I was born at Rigshospitalet, grew up in Vanløse, spent my twenties bouncing between Nørrebro and Vesterbro, and now I live in Frederiksberg with my two kids and a cat who refuses to stay indoors. I've watched this city change — some of it for the better, some of it not — and I've had enough conversations with tourists asking me "where should I actually go?" that I finally decided to write it all down.

This blog is not about the Little Mermaid. It's not about Nyhavn. You already know about those. Google knows about those. Every travel influencer with a Canon camera and a Ryanair ticket knows about those.

This is about the Copenhagen I grew up in. The places I take my friends when they visit. The spots where you'll sit next to a retired schoolteacher, a graphic designer on her lunch break, and a dad pushing a stroller — not a tour group following an umbrella.

Let's start with three of my favourites.

---

## 1. Café Intime — Frederiksberg's Best Kept Secret

**Allegade 25, Frederiksberg**

I have been going to Café Intime since I was old enough to drink coffee unsupervised, and I am convinced that most people who live ten minutes away have never heard of it. That's the whole point.

From the outside it looks like it might be closed, or possibly a private home. There's a small sign, a heavy wooden door, and no queue of people waiting outside with phones out. You push the door open and you walk into what feels genuinely like someone's living room — if that someone had excellent taste in jazz records and didn't believe in overhead lighting.

The interior is all dark wood, mismatched velvet chairs, and shelves crowded with old bottles and framed photographs of musicians. There's a piano in the corner that occasionally gets played. The walls are covered in signed portraits going back decades. It opened in 1796, which makes it one of the oldest cafés in Copenhagen, but it doesn't perform its own history at you — it just exists, quietly, the way old things do when they're confident about themselves.

What to order: They're known for their hot drinks and their selection of spirits, but honestly what you should do is arrive at around 3pm on a weekday when it's half empty, order whatever is warm, and sit near the window that looks out onto the quiet Frederiksberg street. Stay for two hours. Read something. Nobody will rush you.

**Who goes here:** Locals from Frederiksberg, older regulars who've been coming for thirty years, the occasional jazz musician. You will almost certainly be the only tourist in the room, and that is the correct feeling.

**Maja's tip:** Go in autumn or winter. The combination of candlelight, rain on the windows, and whatever is playing softly in the background is one of the best things Copenhagen does.

## 2. Assistens Kirkegård — A Cemetery Where Locals Actually Relax

**Kapelvej 4, Nørrebro**

Yes, I'm recommending a cemetery. Stay with me.

Assistens Kirkegård is where Hans Christian Andersen and Søren Kierkegaard are buried, so it technically appears in guidebooks — but always framed as a cultural pilgrimage, a solemn place to stand in front of a famous headstone and take a respectful photograph. What the guidebooks don't mention is that on a sunny Tuesday afternoon, half of Nørrebro is lying on the grass here eating lunch.

This is genuinely how locals use it. Families spread out blankets between the graves. People bring their dogs. Students sit against old headstones reading paperbacks. It sounds strange if you haven't seen it, but when you're actually there it feels completely natural — peaceful and alive at the same time, if you'll forgive the phrasing.

The cemetery is huge and beautifully maintained. Old trees, gravel paths, sections that are formally arranged and sections that have gone slightly wild in a way that feels intentional. In the spring there are flowering trees and the light through the leaves in the older parts of the cemetery is genuinely one of the most beautiful things I've seen in this city, and I've seen this city in every season for forty years.

**What to do:** Enter from Kapelvej on the Nørrebro side. Walk slowly. If you want to find Andersen or Kierkegaard, you can — maps are available at the entrance. But also just walk without a destination and let yourself drift into the quieter sections. Bring something to eat. Sit down somewhere that feels right.

**Maja's tip:** Come on a weekday in May or June, mid-morning, when the school groups haven't arrived yet and the lunch crowd hasn't gathered. You'll have long stretches of it almost entirely to yourself. On a warm day with the trees in bloom, it is genuinely one of the most beautiful places in the city.

**Closest place to eat nearby:** Walk five minutes to Mimersgade and get something from one of the small bakeries or the Lebanese places on the street. Eat it in the cemetery. I am serious about this.

## 3. Værnedamsvej — The Street That Thinks It's in Paris

**Værnedamsvej, Vesterbro/Frederiksberg border**

Every Copenhagener knows Værnedamsvej but it almost never makes it into tourist guides, probably because it's hard to photograph in a way that communicates what makes it good — which is the atmosphere, not any single thing in it.

It's a short street, maybe 400 metres, that runs along the border between Vesterbro and Frederiksberg. It's lined almost entirely with small independent shops and cafés, and for reasons I've never fully understood it has always had a slightly French character — wine bars, a proper fromagerie, flower shops spilling onto the pavement, the kind of butcher who will tell you exactly which farm the pork came from and why it matters.

I've been coming here my whole life. My mother used to bring me to buy cheese here when I was small and it felt like an event. It still feels like an event, which is rare for a street you've known for decades.

**What to do on Værnedamsvej:**

Stop at **Juice & Java** for coffee in the morning — it's small, the coffee is good, and the regulars who stand outside with their cups are exactly the Vesterbro crowd you'd hope to find. Walk the whole street slowly, looking in the windows. Go into the cheese shop (**La Glace** is nearby on Skoubogade for reference, though that's technically a different detour — worth it for the cream cake). Have a glass of wine somewhere in the late afternoon at one of the wine bars and watch the street slow down as the shops start closing.

The whole experience takes maybe two hours if you're doing it right, which means buying something, eating something, sitting outside with something, and not being in a hurry.

**Maja's tip:** Saturday morning is the best time. The street is busy but not overwhelming, the flower shops have their full stock out, and everything feels slightly celebratory in the way that Saturday mornings do when you live in a city you love. Avoid Sunday — many of the smaller shops are closed.

**Who goes here:** People who live in Vesterbro and Frederiksberg, people who moved to Copenhagen from France and feel at home here, parents with prams, people walking their dogs, couples buying wine for dinner. Very few tourists, and the ones who are there have usually been tipped off by someone who lives here.

## A note on how to use this blog

I'm going to keep writing these. Copenhagen is full of places like this — not hidden exactly, because the people who live nearby know them perfectly well, but invisible to the kind of tourism infrastructure that only surfaces the things that are already famous.

If you're visiting and you want to eat where the locals eat, sit where the locals sit, and walk streets that haven't been optimised for people carrying selfie sticks, this is the blog for you. I'm not a professional writer and I'm not trying to be. I'm just a woman who has lived in this city her entire life and thinks it deserves to be seen properly.
